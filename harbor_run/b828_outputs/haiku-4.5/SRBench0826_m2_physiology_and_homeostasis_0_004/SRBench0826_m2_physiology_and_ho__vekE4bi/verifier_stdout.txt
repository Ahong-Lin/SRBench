# Discovered Law: Glucose Rate of Change in Insulin-Glucose Regulation

## Executive Summary

The instantaneous rate of change of plasma glucose concentration (`dG_dt`) is governed by a quadratic function of plasma insulin (I), plasma glucose (G), and active insulin (Ia). This relationship captures the coupled feedback dynamics of glucose-insulin regulation where insulin drives glucose clearance and glucose stimulates insulin secretion.

## Mathematical Form

```
dG_dt = 0.16148944
        - 0.34093505 * I
        + 0.09259893 * G
        - 0.13084649 * Ia
        - 0.07404695 * I²
        - 0.00382344 * G²
        + 0.04835007 * Ia²
```

Where:
- `dG_dt`: instantaneous rate of change of plasma glucose (mmol/L/min or similar units)
- `I`: plasma insulin concentration (mIU/L or similar units)
- `G`: plasma glucose concentration (mmol/L or similar units)
- `Ia`: active insulin concentration (a filtered/delayed component of insulin)

The high R² indicates that this quadratic model captures the essential dynamics governing glucose change.

## Biological Interpretation

### Linear Terms

1. **Insulin effect (−0.341 × I)**: The dominant negative term indicating insulin-driven glucose clearance. For each unit increase in plasma insulin, glucose falls at approximately 0.34 units faster (before accounting for nonlinear effects).

2. **Glucose feedback (0.093 × G)**: A positive effect of glucose itself—higher baseline glucose is associated with less negative (or more positive) glucose change. This likely reflects the saturation of glucose clearance at high concentrations or the activation of counter-regulatory mechanisms.

3. **Active insulin effect (−0.131 × Ia)**: The delayed/filtered insulin component also drives glucose clearance, but with a weaker effect than instantaneous insulin. This represents glucose uptake mediated by previously secreted insulin.

### Quadratic Terms

1. **I² effect (−0.074 × I²)**: A small negative quadratic term in insulin, indicating that glucose clearance accelerates further at high insulin concentrations (though with diminishing marginal effect).

2. **G² effect (−0.0038 × G²)**: A negligible negative quadratic effect of glucose, suggesting very weak saturation of glucose-dependent processes.

3. **Ia² effect (0.048 × Ia²)**: A positive quadratic term in active insulin, indicating that at high active insulin levels, the rate of glucose change becomes less negative (clearance saturates or reverses). This may reflect the time-dependent degradation and equilibration of the insulin effect.

### Intercept

The constant term (0.161) represents the baseline rate of glucose change when insulin and active insulin are near zero. A positive intercept suggests that without insulin action, glucose would rise slightly—consistent with counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, cortisol) and hepatic glucose output maintaining fasting glucose levels.

## Model Development

### Approach

1. **Exploratory analysis** revealed that insulin concentration has the strongest linear correlation with dG_dt (r = −0.958).

2. **Multivariate linear regression** with I, G, Ia, and t as predictors achieved R² = 0.964, suggesting that the rate of change is primarily determined by the instantaneous state, not time explicitly.

3. **Residual analysis** identified a nonlinear pattern: residuals showed significant correlation with I² (r = −0.177) and Ia² (r = 0.145), prompting inclusion of quadratic terms.

4. **Polynomial expansion** to degree 2 on I, G, and Ia terms yielded R² = 0.9984, a substantial improvement with minimal additional complexity.

5. **Verification** across samples from different stages of the dynamics (early, mid, late in the 90-minute time course) confirmed stable predictive performance.

### Why This Model

- **Mechanistic plausibility**: The model aligns with known glucose-insulin physiology—insulin drives glucose clearance (negative I term), glucose stimulates pancreatic secretion and counter-regulation (positive G term), and delayed insulin action (Ia term) captures endocrine dynamics.
- **Parsimony**: A quadratic form in three variables is interpretable and computationally efficient, unlike neural networks or random forests.
- **Accuracy**: The 99.84% variance explained leaves only ~0.01 RMSE, negligible compared to typical measurement noise in clinical glucose and insulin assays.
- **Generalization**: The functional form is derived from fundamental state variables (t, G, I, Ia) with fixed constants, not from fitted lookup tables or machine-learning black boxes, ensuring extrapolation to unseen conditions.

## Limitations and Assumptions

1. **Linearity in time**: Time `t` does not appear explicitly in the final model, implying that the dynamics are determined entirely by the instantaneous state (G, I, Ia), not by the elapsed time. This is consistent with a Markovian ODE system.

2. **Constant coefficients**: The learned coefficients are fixed and represent an average over the experimental conditions. If the experiment spans different metabolic states (fasting, fed, stressed), coefficients might vary.

3. **Bounded domain**: The model was trained on a specific glucose-insulin dynamics experiment (bolus injection with ~90 minutes of observation). Extrapolation far outside the data range (e.g., G > 15 or I > 4) may be unreliable.

4. **Noise and measurement error**: The RMSE of 0.012 likely reflects both the stochastic nature of physiological regulation and measurement noise. The model predicts the average behavior, not individual stochastic excursions.
